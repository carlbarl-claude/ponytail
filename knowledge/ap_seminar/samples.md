# AP Seminar — Illustrative Scored Samples

> **These are original, synthetic teaching examples — NOT official College Board
> student samples.** They illustrate *why* a response is strong or weak against the
> AP Seminar skills. For real released samples, see `knowledge/sources.md` and upload
> them to the bot.

## Research question — strong vs. weak
**Weak:** "Is social media bad?"
- *Why it's weak:* Too broad, yes/no framed, not researchable, and value-loaded
  ("bad" for whom, at what, measured how?). It invites opinion, not inquiry.

**Strong:** "To what extent does adolescent social-media use affect self-reported
anxiety, and how do psychological and design-based perspectives explain that link?"
- *Why it's strong:* Focused, researchable, names an outcome that can be
  investigated, and explicitly invites **multiple perspectives** (psychology vs.
  interface design) — which the rubric rewards.

## Source evaluation — earns credit vs. not
**Earns credit:** "This 2023 peer-reviewed study in a psychology journal is credible
for my question because the authors are researchers with no commercial stake
(authority, purpose), it's recent enough to reflect current platforms (currency),
and it directly measures anxiety and usage (relevance). Its limitation is a small
sample, so I treat its findings as suggestive, not conclusive."
- *Why:* It applies credibility criteria *and* weighs a limitation — that's genuine
  evaluation, not summary.

**Does not earn credit:** "I found a website that says social media causes anxiety,
so that supports my point."
- *Why it fails:* No evaluation of who published it, when, why, or how reliable it
  is. Naming a source isn't evaluating it.

## Line of reasoning / synthesis — strong excerpt
"Although the psychological perspective attributes rising anxiety to social
comparison, and the design perspective attributes it to engagement-maximizing
features, these are not competing explanations so much as linked ones: platform
design *amplifies* the comparison psychologists describe. My argument is therefore
that interventions targeting design (e.g., removing visible like-counts) may reduce
anxiety more effectively than telling teens to 'compare less,' because they address
the mechanism rather than the symptom."
- *Why it's strong:* It doesn't just list two views — it **synthesizes** them into
  the student's *own* argument with a clear line of reasoning, and acknowledges
  complexity. That is the heart of what AP Seminar rewards.

## Oral defense — a good habit
When asked "Why did you choose these particular sources?", a strong answer names the
selection *criteria* ("I wanted one empirical study, one industry-insider account,
and one critical perspective, so I could triangulate credibility") rather than just
re-describing the sources. Graders reward reflective reasoning about your *process*.

## Common attribution mistake
Paraphrasing a source's idea without any in-text attribution — even if unintentional
— hurts the conventions score and risks an academic-integrity issue. Every borrowed
idea or piece of evidence needs a clear in-text citation in your chosen style
(MLA/APA), not just a bibliography entry at the end.
