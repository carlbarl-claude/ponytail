# AP Seminar — Scoring Rubrics (summary)

Plain-language summaries of what AP Seminar assessments reward. AP Seminar is scored
across three components: two through-course Performance Tasks and one end-of-course
exam. For exact official rubric wording, see `knowledge/sources.md`.

## What each rubric row generally measures
Across the written and presentation tasks, AP Seminar rubrics reward the same core
skills (each scored on a multi-point scale, typically 0–6):
- **Question & explore:** frame a focused, researchable question or problem.
- **Understand & analyze:** accurately summarize and analyze others' arguments,
  including their line of reasoning and evidence.
- **Evaluate multiple perspectives:** identify, compare, and weigh several
  credible perspectives on the issue — not just two opposing sides.
- **Evaluate sources & evidence:** judge credibility, relevance, and bias
  (currency, authority, purpose, accuracy), and use evidence appropriately.
- **Synthesize / build an argument:** establish your *own* well-reasoned argument
  or conclusion that connects perspectives, and acknowledge complexity/limitations.
- **Communicate / conventions:** organize clearly, engage the audience, and use
  accurate attribution and citation (MLA or APA).

## Performance Task 1 — Team Project and Presentation
- **IRR (Individual Research Report), ~1200 words:** each student researches one
  perspective/lens on the team's problem. Rewards: a clear research question,
  well-chosen credible sources, accurate analysis of perspectives, and proper
  citation. It should read as rigorous individual research, not opinion.
- **TMP (Team Multimedia Presentation) + defense:** the team presents a solution or
  resolution; individuals answer an oral defense question. Rewards: a coherent team
  argument, effective use of evidence and media, and the ability to defend reasoning
  on the spot.

## Performance Task 2 — Individual Research-Based Essay and Presentation
- **IWA (Individual Written Argument), ~2000 words:** from released stimulus
  material, you develop your *own* research question and argument. Rewards, roughly
  one rubric row each:
  1. Contextualize the problem and situate your question.
  2. Identify, compare, and evaluate **multiple perspectives**.
  3. Evaluate the **credibility and relevance** of evidence.
  4. Establish an argument with a clear **line of reasoning** and your own conclusion.
  5. Select and **integrate evidence** effectively to support the argument.
  6. Use **conventions**: organization, style, grammar, and accurate attribution.
- **IMP (Individual Multimedia Presentation) + oral defense:** present your argument
  and answer defense questions.

## End-of-Course Exam
- **Part A (Analyze an argument):** short-answer questions on a single source.
  Reward: accurately identify the author's **argument, line of reasoning, and
  evidence**, and evaluate the effectiveness of that reasoning.
- **Part B (Build an argument):** using **four** provided sources, construct your own
  evidence-based argument on a common theme. Reward: a defensible thesis, use of at
  least a required number of the sources, logical reasoning, and attribution.

## Source-credibility checklist (the "CRAAP-style" lens graders reward)
- **Currency:** how recent is it, and does recency matter for this topic?
- **Relevance:** does it actually address *your* question?
- **Authority:** who wrote it, and what are their credentials or stake?
- **Accuracy:** is it evidence-based, verifiable, and free of obvious error?
- **Purpose:** why was it created — to inform, persuade, sell, or provoke? What bias
  does that introduce?
